## 5.5 What Was Not Confirmed, What Was Rejected, and Why It Matters

**Proposed and not confirmed.**

- **That tier-indexed expected-loss verdicts reduce financial harm at acceptable cost in operation.** This was the central practical claim of the design. It needs enforcement with live agents, which was not run. It remains unestablished.
- **That the four verdicts are useful as four.** Modify, Escalate and Block coincide in replay without a reviewer.
- **That behavioral evidence helps.** It did not. Removing it raised task completion, and that gain met the pre-declared decision rule.
- **That calibration improves decisions.** Removing it left the attack count unchanged and five more benign tasks were completed.
- **That consequence-aware learned decisions add value over simple rules.** No such value was established, for REM or for a learned model given task-permission evidence.
- **That the two-verdict decision is safe by itself.** Applied to REM v1.1 without a guard it released every held-out credential-change attack.

**Why the negative findings matter.**

1. **They test published proposals under a condition their authors did not examine.** Supervision on intervention value improves control in the settings studied by C. Zhang et al. (2026). Here, with nine attack objectives and one of them held out, it removed protection against the held-out class. A method that depends on outcome labels inherits the coverage of those labels.
2. **They show what a strong security figure can hide.** REM v1.0's attack success of 0.4% coexists with the release of 4 of 89 legitimate transfers. Reported without the second figure, the first would describe a policy that withholds payments as if it were a policy that detects attacks. This is the over-defense that T. Yang et al. (2026) report in general.
3. **They place the difficulty in what the evidence represents.** With provenance evidence, a good estimator, a simple rule and an elaborate policy converge on the same frontier and fail the same paired cases. In the exploratory extension, a deterministic representation of the request moved the frontier on development data, and learned or calibrated decisions on top of it changed nothing. Effort on the estimator is therefore unlikely to help; a trusted statement of what the user authorized, or a validated way of compiling it from the request, might.
4. **They qualify provenance as evidence.** Exact-string provenance is removed by reformatting an identifier, and a document that the user names is exactly where an attacker writes.

## 5.6 Limitations and Threats to Validity

The limitations are discussed in Chapter 4, Section 4.6.11. In summary:

1. **Replay, not enforcement.** Every outcome assumes that the agent emits its recorded calls whatever is withheld. A live agent can retry or take another route. Replay attack success is not an upper bound on live attack success.
2. **One benchmark.** One environment, sixteen user tasks, nine attack objectives, one attacker account. The second population has new agents and templates and the same tasks. A few tasks decide each utility comparison.
3. **Design status.** Costs, tiers, predicates, the feature set and the label rule are declared values without approval. Every result is exploratory by label.
4. **Labels.** They are reference-matched attempt labels, ambiguous for one user task in early benchmark versions, and not audited by a human.
5. **No reviewer.** Escalation outcomes are bounded by an oracle and by no reviewer. No reviewer effectiveness is assumed or measured.
6. **Reuse and post-hoc analysis.** The primary recordings were examined many times. Only one contrast was tested on data unread at the time, and those data are development evidence since then. The extension study has no confirmatory part, and its request parser was written with the benchmark's requests in view.
7. **Statistics.** Nine attack groups and sixteen task groups; intervals are wide and need not reach nominal coverage; one run per configuration.
8. **Missing component.** No injection classifier was available for the Detection Layer.
9. **Literature.** The systematic review was conducted at record level. Six papers were read in full afterwards. Four close works are known only from abstracts or from their authors' repositories (NEXUS, aiAuthZ, Kairos, CaMeL); the Progent paper was read in full. PACT, ROPE and CapLite (November 2026) are known from abstracts, ROPE also from its repository. The papers of the three published methods tested behind the permission guard were not readable; their authors' or library implementations were used.
10. **Limited external comparison.** One published deterministic policy was replayed on the recorded episodes, decided by its authors' own code. Its list of recipients is informed by the benchmark. The four distributed defenses are compared through their authors' recorded runs with one model, against REM's replay, which is descriptive. DRIFT and MELON, which need a language model, were compared at the level of architecture and code and executed only with scripted agents. REM was not run in a closed loop, and the tool_filter traces could not be replayed with the frozen harness.
11. **Fixtures and rules were written with knowledge of the benchmark and of REM's features.**
12. **Granularity of the layer ablation.** The Input & Context layer could not be removed on its own, and the Detection layer's classifier is absent. A component without a measurable effect here is not shown to be unnecessary.

## 5.7 Future Work, in Order of Priority

1. **Supervisor decisions.** Approve or replace the declared design values, the label rule, the mitigation semantics, the replay amendment and the proposed amendment REM v1.1 (Appendix B).
2. **A trusted authorization input.** Specify how a structured statement of what the user authorized (operation, source account, recipient, amount, constraints) is captured by the trusted interface, and check proposed actions against it deterministically. It fits in the Input & Context Layer and as a policy predicate of the Decision Engine; no sixth layer is needed. It changes the threat model by adding a trusted channel, and its evaluation needs a benchmark that contains such records. The present thesis shows that the tested provenance features do not replace it. The exploratory permission rule of Chapter 4, Section 4.6.9, is a first step that compiles such a statement from the request text. It needs requests written and reviewed by other people (the authoring instructions and a sealed scorer are in the repository), a decision on subjective conditions, and a live-agent evaluation.
3. **Live enforcing-mode evaluation.** Run the no-defense reference, B1, both REM versions and the rule baselines with live agents, with at least three repeated runs and fixed, recorded versions and decoding parameters. This measures adaptation after refusals and whether the four verdicts differ.
4. **Comparison with published defenses under the same conditions.** A sealed protocol fixes one benchmark version for all conditions and names the comparisons in advance. The MELON integration is ready and needs a model credential, MELON's embedding service and an approved spending limit. DRIFT's official code already runs on the benchmark version used here with a scripted agent. A live run needs a model credential, an approved budget and a decision on the benchmark version, because DRIFT's defaults and its authors' runs use banking v1.2. Progent with generated policies and Task Shield need a model as well. A comparison with NEXUS needs its full text, its code at a pinned commit and a justified adapter.
5. **Label audit.** Annotate the prepared package with at least one human annotator, preferably two, and report agreement.
6. **A reviewer model** with error rate and delay, so that the value of Escalate can be estimated between its two bounds.
7. **Provenance that survives reformatting,** by canonicalizing identifiers before matching. The extension applies a type-specific canonical form inside the permission rule; the provenance labeler of REM itself is unchanged, and adaptive variants were not tested.
8. **The injection classifier,** selected on the agent's own tool outputs at a low false-positive rate (Z. Liu, 2026).
9. **Full-text literature review,** including the three works known only from abstracts, and a search of the named databases.
10. **Further benchmarks,** with new user tasks, reported per benchmark.
11. **Independent validation of the amount verifier** on payment requests written and reviewed by others, with half of the accounts taken from Progent's list so that the comparison tests amounts and not recipients (`REM_REPAIR_STUDY_2026_11_17/07_INDEPENDENT_VALIDATION_KIT.md`).
12. **Direct prompt injection.** Evaluate the prompt-channel adversary AD-2 (Chapter 3, Section 3.3.4), which is in the design scope but is not exercised by the evaluated banking attacks. The benchmark's attack template named `direct` places its text in tool outputs and is therefore an indirect injection. REM's provenance labeling ranks the user's input above tool outputs (Section 3.4.2), so an adversary who controls the user channel needs other evidence, such as an authorization statement captured outside that channel (item 2), together with an amended threat model, a label rule for attacks that originate in the user channel, and a benchmark that contains such attacks.
13. **Goal drift.** Evaluate an agent's tendency to deviate from its original objective over time (Arike et al., 2025), which can occur without an adversary. The label of Section 3.3.5 covers only adversarially induced actions and excludes the agent's own errors, so this needs a new label definition (a supervisor decision), behavioral features fitted to drift labels, and an environment that records such labels.

## 5.8 Conclusion

This thesis set out to design a runtime framework that decides, for each tool call proposed by a financial AI agent, whether to allow, modify, escalate or block it. The decision minimizes expected loss under a calibrated probability of adversarial inducement and a declared consequence tier, and every verdict is recorded for audit. The framework was specified, implemented at the tool boundary of a benchmark agent, and evaluated on 5,426 recorded episodes of real agents in a banking environment: 3,313 used for fitting and cross-validated evaluation, and 2,113 held back for one confirmatory test and used as development evidence afterwards.

Four things were found.

**REM is safe in replay, and restrictive.** It withholds nearly all recorded attack attempts, on attack objectives, user tasks, agents and templates it has not seen. It does so largely by withholding state-changing calls, legitimate ones included.

**Its learned and cost-based parts are not shown to earn their place.** Rules that read one or two pieces of provenance evidence reach the same security with at least as much completed work. Within the framework, one change raises task completion, and that gain was confirmed on data unread at the time: removing behavioral evidence. That change brings REM to the level of the rules, not above it.

**The limit is what the evidence represents.** Every policy built on provenance evidence, simple or elaborate, fails when an unrequested payment uses the user's own words, and an elaborate policy trained on outcomes failed on an attack class it had not seen. These are findings about the tested features and policies, not a proof that the request text cannot supply the missing information.

**A deterministic representation of the request changed the measured trade-off; the learned core contributed nothing measurable behind it.** In an exploratory extension, a rule that compiles the user's request into instructions and releases a state-changing call only if it matches one had no successful attack on development data and completed more benign tasks than REM v1.0 in point estimate. Against the strongest simple rule it had fewer successful attacks, with the difference in completed tasks not resolved. A published fixed list of payees also had no successful attack. Two external reviews found defects in the matching, and the repairs removed the remaining attacks without losing tasks, except in the one task whose subjective condition the rule declines to treat as satisfied. Behind its authority guard, a learned model, calibrated thresholds and boosted trees gave exactly the outcomes of the guard used alone (GV), except one threshold that lost tasks. The reason is that the guard stopped every attack-labeled call before any of these components was consulted. This is not evidence that a learner can never help, since the recordings contain no attack that passes the rule. All of these security differences arise in one benchmark task, and the rule has not been tested on requests written by anyone but its author. An external review then showed that the guard accepted computed amounts it never computed. A typed verifier repairs this; the price is that payments whose amount the request does not determine are withheld.

Two pre-registered ablations settle where REM v1.0's own protection comes from. The learned, consequence-aware decision is not idle in REM v1.0: it decides almost every call and accounts for all 661 attacks that REM v1.0 stops (of 672 reached without defense), with none admitted. It does so by withholding broadly. Calibration, Modify, the predicates and context evidence change no attack outcome. Across the five layers, every measured outcome effect comes from that decision acting through enforcement. In shadow mode the same verdicts protect nothing, and the Input & Context layer's labels matter through the action/provenance evidence built on them. Against the four defenses distributed with the benchmark, REM's replayed decisions leave fewer successful attacks than three of them and about as many as an injection classifier, at a lower completion of benign tasks. Because their runs are closed-loop and REM's is a replay, this places REM among them without ranking it. Progent's published banking policy, decided by Progent's own code on the same recorded episodes, admits no attack and completes more tasks under attack than REM on all three populations. Its list of permitted recipients is informed by the benchmark, so this, too, is not a ranking.

These findings come from recorded traces and from a replay that assumes the agent does not adapt. They do not show that REM protects a live agent, and they do not rank REM against any published defense. Their value is narrower and, within its bounds, firm: a transparent instance of a widely proposed design was built, compared fairly with the simplest alternatives, and found to be no better than them on the evidence available at the tool boundary. Where an improvement was observed, it came from a deterministic rule and not from the learned, consequence-aware decision that the thesis set out to evaluate. The thesis reports that result as it was measured.
