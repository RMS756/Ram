### S-2.2.2 Information sources {.unnumbered}

The review protocol named eleven preferred sources:

- IEEE Xplore;
- ACM Digital Library;
- Scopus or Web of Science;
- ScienceDirect;
- SpringerLink;
- ACL Anthology;
- NeurIPS and PMLR proceedings;
- ICLR proceedings;
- USENIX;
- arXiv, as a supplementary source.

These sources were not directly searchable from the review environment. Direct access was attempted on 25 September 2026, and the network policy refused every connection. The connection log is retained with the search record. An attempt to use Elicit, a second scholarly index, also failed: the account's plan does not include API access. Each of these sources is therefore recorded as **DATABASE ACCESS UNAVAILABLE** (`CHAPTER2_SYSTEMATIC_SEARCH_STRATEGY.md`, §1).

**Source actually searched.** The one bibliographic database searched was **OpenAlex**, through a literature-search tool that queries its index of about 250 million records. OpenAlex indexes the proceedings and journals of the publishers listed above, as well as arXiv. Studies from those venues could therefore be retrieved, but only through OpenAlex's index and relevance ranking, not through each library's own search interface. The review accordingly does **not** claim to have searched the named digital libraries.

**Other methods.** A second, non-database stream was used, as PRISMA 2020 allows. It consists of the 70 sources in the reference list of the previous integrated version of this chapter. That list was assembled by known-item searching of the thesis corpus and backward citation checking, and it was verified between 16 and 25 September 2026. Crossref was used only to verify metadata, never to identify records.

### S-2.2.3 Search strategy {.unnumbered}

**Concept blocks.** The protocol defines three concept blocks and a financial block:

- **A — Agents:** "LLM agent", "AI agent", "language model agent", "tool-using agent", "autonomous agent";
- **B — Security:** "security", "prompt injection", "indirect prompt injection", "tool misuse", "agent safety", "goal hijacking", "memory poisoning", "action manipulation";
- **C — Runtime, behavior and mitigation:** "runtime monitoring", "runtime interception", "behavioral monitoring", "trajectory", "tool use", "provenance", "anomaly detection", "mitigation", "guardrail", "policy enforcement";
- **Financial:** "financial agent", "banking agent", "financial AI agent", "financial tool", "financial transaction", combined with security, prompt-injection, tool-misuse, runtime, safety or mitigation terms.

**Database variant.** The OpenAlex interface available to the review is lexical and relevance-ranked, and it does not accept Boolean operators. The blocks were therefore translated into eleven free-text queries, each combining at least one term from blocks A, B and C. Two queries (Q7, Q8) carried the financial block. The exact strings are reproduced in Table A.1. Each query was restricted to publication years 2019–2026. Page 2 was also retrieved for the two queries with the highest page-1 yield of eligible records (Q1, Q2), giving 13 result pages and 320 records in total.

The final search date was **25 September 2026**, and every counted search ran on that date. The raw output of every query is stored unchanged, and a script recomputes all counts from it (`chapter2/slr/`).

**Retrieval cap.** Only the relevance-ranked top of each query was screened: 24–25 records per page, the maximum the tool returned. The index estimates show that far more records matched the query terms. This cap is a deliberate feasibility decision, consistent with the protocol's instruction not to screen thousands of records manually. It is also the review's largest search limitation (Section 2.12.4). The stopping rule, the tool's handling of each string, and a one-off pilot probe that is not counted are all documented in the search record.

**Eligibility.** A study was included when it met six criteria fixed before assessment: it concerns tool-using AI agents or LLM-integrated applications that ingest untrusted content; it addresses a security, safety, robustness, monitoring or misuse problem; it makes a substantive technical or empirical contribution; it informs at least one dimension relevant to REM; it gives enough information to extract that contribution; and it was published between January 2019 and 25 September 2026, or is a necessary foundational source. Secondary studies were not synthesized as primary evidence. The full criteria and the exclusion labels are in Appendix A.

### S-2.8.4 Other decision mechanisms {.unnumbered}

**Jackson (2025).** A working paper (LIMITED EVIDENCE; PARTLY UNVERIFIED) describes a policy engine in which a multi-dimensional risk score drives a deterministic enforcement state machine with allow, deny, sanitize and escalate decisions. Its decision set corresponds closely to REM's four verdicts. It is unrefereed, its full text was not accessed, and its evaluation claims are qualitative.

**Consequence-aware control.**

- H.-H. Chen (2026; preprint) prices each side-effect-bearing action, such as a payment or refund, against a safe default and gates it against a reserve capital budget. It does not estimate how likely the action is to have been induced.
- SafeAgent's decision core instantiates operators for risk encoding, utility-cost evaluation, consequence modeling and policy arbitration over persistent session state (H. Liu et al., 2026; level A). Whether these operators are realized through LLM reasoning is unverified (level U).
- NEXUS encodes consequence in plan annotations and rules.

**Confidence-based escalation.** LATTICE separates deciding from judging, so that no component both proposes and approves an action. It escalates to human operators on confidence, and reports zero unsafe actions across four planner families (Calboreanu, 2026). It achieved this at a conservative operating point that auto-allowed no action. In the same study, a confidence-threshold baseline's false-allow rate varied from 0.03 to 0.998 across planners.

**Transactional and reversible mitigation.**

- Cordon stages outward-facing effects in an outbox and executes reversible mutations in shadow state (Z. Chen et al., 2026).
- GoEX offers undo and damage confinement for post-facto validation (Patil et al., 2024).

Such substrates *consume* a decision and could supply the mechanics of a hold or undo response.

### S-2.9.1 General agent-security evidence with a financial component {.unnumbered}

Several included general studies contain a financial element but report no finance-specific analysis in the verified record:

- **AgentDojo** includes an executable Banking suite (Debenedetti et al., 2024).
- **ASB** includes finance as one of ten scenarios (H. Zhang et al., 2025).
- **AgentHarm** includes fraud among eleven harm categories (Andriushchenko et al., 2025).
- **ClawSafety** includes finance among five professional domains. Its scenarios include redirecting financial transactions (Wei et al., 2026).
- **ToolEmu** cites financial loss as a risk example (Ruan et al., 2024).
- **RTBAS** cites financial transactions as a motivating example (Zhong et al., 2025).

These studies establish that financial actions are within the attack surface that general benchmarks cover. They do not establish how defenses perform on financial actions specifically.

## S-2.10 Comparative Evidence Synthesis {.unnumbered}

Two tables compare the included runtime defense and design studies. Both are built from the systematic evidence matrix (`CHAPTER2_SYSTEMATIC_EVIDENCE_MATRIX.csv`).

- **Table A.5** (Appendix A) records what each study observes and does, using the columns specified in the review protocol.
- **Table A.8** records how the studies closest to REM turn evidence into a decision.

**Coding.** Yes = reported in the verified record; NR = Not Reported; NV = Not Verified; NA = Not Applicable. A property that the record does not report is **not** inferred to be present or absent. Financial context is coded Yes only for a primarily financial setting. Evidence levels are HIGH, MOD (moderate) and LIM (limited), as defined in Section 2.2.5.

**Table A.5** (42 defense and design studies × 13 properties) is reproduced in **Appendix A**. In summary: 41 of the 42 studies operate at runtime, 34 code tool/action evidence, 21 multi-step handling, 17 behavioral evidence and 8 provenance evidence as present in the record; these counts describe what the records report (Section 2.2.5), not what the systems lack.

**Reading the tables.**

*Row by row*, every individual property in REM's design appears somewhere in the included studies:

- calibrated logistic scoring with a four-way intervention set: NEXUS;
- pre-execution interception with graduated verdicts and human review: AgentTrust, RTBAS, LATTICE;
- consequence-dependent control: H.-H. Chen, NEXUS, SafeAgent;
- provenance and cross-step information-flow evidence: ProvenanceGuard, CaMeL, ACE, RTBAS, the MCP policy-enforcement point;
- trajectory-aware monitoring: MI9, PRISM, ProbGuard, DreamGuard, ToolSafe, AgentTrust;
- latency and component ablation: AgentTrust, NEXUS, the MCP policy-enforcement point;
- a financial runtime harness: FinHarness;
- the limits of calibration and confidence thresholds for control: C. Zhang et al.; LATTICE.

*Column by column*, the combinations thin out:

- **NEXUS** alone reports a calibrated probability together with costs and a graduated response. It does so at the plan level, on author-generated templates, with fixed intervention costs setting thresholds inside a rule-first cascade.
- **Provenance evidence**, where its contribution has been measured, feeds rules (the MCP policy-enforcement point) or a detector (ProvenanceGuard), not a probability estimator.
- **Financial focus** appears only in LIMITED EVIDENCE studies.
- **No included record** reports all three of the following together:
  - a calibrated probability of adversarial induction per proposed tool call;
  - consequence-dependent losses used to select among graduated responses;
  - evaluation on an executable financial environment with utility, intervention behavior and latency reported.

  This observation concerns the *records* examined. A combination this specific is easy to miss in a record-level review and is weak evidence of originality on its own (Section 2.12.2).

Table A.8, reproduced in Appendix A, records for the runtime systems closest to REM the decision basis, whether a calibrated probability is produced, whether consequence enters the decision, the human-review and graduated responses, and which evaluation dimensions are reported.

### S-2.12.1 Assessment of candidate gaps {.unnumbered}

The review protocol listed five candidate gaps, A to E, to be retained only if the systematic evidence supported them. Their assessment is in Appendix A (Table A.10). In summary, the review did not support a gap in the existence of runtime interception, graduated responses, calibration or consequence awareness; it supported narrowed versions of three gaps concerning evidence: on the value of behavioral and provenance evidence as input to a calibrated estimator (Gap C), on runtime defenses for financial actions in an open, executable benchmark (Gap D), and on a comparison of a consequence-aware decision with a consequence-independent baseline on the same estimator (Gap E).

### S-2.12.4 Limitations of this review {.unnumbered}

The review is systematic in procedure but **not exhaustive**, and its conclusions are bounded as follows.

1. **Database accessibility.** Only OpenAlex was searched. IEEE Xplore, the ACM DL, Scopus and Web of Science, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, the ACL Anthology, NeurIPS/PMLR, ICLR/OpenReview, USENIX and arXiv could not be reached (DATABASE ACCESS UNAVAILABLE). Elicit access was also unavailable. Their content was reached only through OpenAlex's index.
2. **Index coverage, ranking and retrieval cap.** OpenAlex's lexical relevance ranking surfaced general surveys ahead of specific recent preprints. Only the top 24–25 records per query page were screened, although index estimates ran to thousands. The database search retrieved only 3 of the 13 named prior-art studies, which shows that relevant studies can be missed.
3. **Non-independent second stream.** Thirty-one included studies came from the reference list of the previous chapter, which reflects the author's prior reading. This introduces selection bias, which the protocol's requirement to treat named prior art identically mitigates but does not remove.
4. **No full texts.** Eligibility, extraction and appraisal were performed on records; no full-text screening was performed and no claim is at level F. Nine included studies are PARTLY UNVERIFIED. Appraisal items that depend on full text (reproducibility, comparators, limitations) are mostly Not Verified. A supplementary record check on 28 October 2026 re-examined the closest studies (NEXUS, AgentTrust, ProvenanceGuard, FinHarness, FinVault, SafeAgent, C. Zhang et al., H.-H. Chen): it found a peer-reviewed version of ProvenanceGuard (ASE 2026), a changed title for C. Zhang et al., and several details of NEXUS and AgentTrust that are not supported by their current abstracts, which are now marked level U. Full texts remained unreachable.
5. **Single reviewer, AI-assisted.** Screening, extraction and appraisal were each done by one reviewer, assisted by an AI coding assistant, with no independent second screener and no agreement statistic. The appraisal checklist is transparent but is not a validated risk-of-bias instrument.
6. **Rapidly changing preprint literature.** 35 of the 71 included studies are preprints or working papers, and the field produces new preprints monthly. One source (FinVault) was recorded as withdrawn by its authors for non-scientific reasons. Preprint results may change or be withdrawn.
7. **Publication bias.** Defenses are usually reported on the authors' own benchmarks with favorable configurations. Negative results appear mainly in attack papers.
8. **Heterogeneity and no pooling.** Architectures, threats, benchmarks and metrics differ too much for meta-analysis. The synthesis is qualitative, and numeric comparisons across studies are avoided.
9. **Metadata errors in the index.** One record was corrupted (AgentDojo's DOI attached to another abstract) and one venue year was inconsistent (PromptShield). Both were detected and recorded, but others may have gone unnoticed.

**Before submission.** The protocol's Boolean blocks should be run directly in at least IEEE Xplore, the ACM DL, Scopus or Web of Science, and arXiv. Full texts of the included studies should be appraised, and the PRISMA flow and gap assessment updated.

### S-3.1.4 Scope {.unnumbered}

**In scope:**
- A single LLM-based agent that invokes tools with generated arguments.
- A financial (banking) task environment with executable tools and state-based checks.
- Indirect prompt injection through tool outputs as the evaluated attack class.
- Runtime mediation of each proposed tool call before it is released.
- *[Proposed scope amendment, pending supervisor approval]* A recorded-trace replay sensitivity analysis under a stated no-adaptation assumption (Section 3.11.10). It is reported as exploratory evidence about fixed recorded trajectories, not as an estimate of enforcement against live or adaptive agents.

**Out of scope:**
- Training-time defenses and modification of the agent's model or prompts.
- Multi-agent systems.
- Evaluation against adaptive adversaries who tune attacks to REM.
- Production deployment in a financial institution.
- Counterfactual estimation of intervention value for adaptive agents, for example by replaying agent prefixes and executing alternative actions from the same state (C. Zhang et al., 2026).

### S-3.11.8 Pilot E0 {.unnumbered}

Before the feature set is frozen, a pilot run on the fitting portions of the outer folds checks feasibility:

- the number of positive steps per backbone and attack template;
- the ROC-AUC of each evidence group alone and combined.

The pilot's decision values are project decisions declared before the pilot, not literature-derived thresholds [pending supervisor approval]:
- the minimum number of positive steps needed to proceed;
- the minimum group ROC-AUC needed to retain an evidence group.

If the positive-step count is insufficient, additional attack templates or backbones are added before the main experiments. The pilot never uses held-out fold predictions. Because every outer fold's held-out data belong to the fitting portions of the other folds, a single global decision taken from all folds' pilot results would use each fold's held-out data indirectly. The retention rule is therefore applied per fold, to that fold's fitting portion only.

### S-3.12.4 Outcome layers and additional metrics {.unnumbered}

A single attack-success figure merges events that occur at different points between an injection and a harm. The analyses added in November 2026 report these layers separately and never combine them into one score.

**Table S-3.16. Outcome layers and the quantity reported for each**

| Layer | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Injection exposure | attacked or benign episode |
| Attacker-induced proposal | calls with the attempt label y = 1 |
| Runtime verdict | Allow, Modify, Escalate or Block for each call |
| Actual execution | call released or withheld |
| Released attack-labelled call | **rate of episodes with a released attack-labelled call**: the share of attacked episodes in which at least one call with y = 1 was released. It does not depend on the benchmark's success checker. It is a proxy: the label marks an attempt, and the effect of the released call on the bank state is not measured separately, so the quantity is not called a committed unauthorized state |
| Attack success | the benchmark's security check (ASR, Table 3.15) |
| Legitimate-task completion | the benchmark's utility check, in benign episodes and under attack |
| Legitimate-action approval | **authorized-call release**: the share released among calls to tools in 𝒯~R~ proposed in benign episodes |

Two further quantities describe burden: the number of Escalate verdicts and of episodes that contain one, and the number of episodes in which any call is withheld. No effectiveness of a reviewer is assumed in any of them.

The benchmark's success check is not treated as a general definition of financial harm; the rate of episodes with a released attack-labelled call is reported beside it. A policy that withholds every sensitive operation cannot appear successful under this reporting, because its legitimate-task completion and authorized-call release are shown in the same table as its attack success.

## S-3.15 System Implementation {.unnumbered}

This section summarizes how the specification is realized in software and how the software was verified. The details are in Appendix B: the environment, the code organization, the implementation of each layer, the test suite, the correspondence between requirements, equations, functions, tests and result files, and the measurement of processing time.

**Implementation rules.** Three rules governed the implementation.

1. **Fail closed.** A design value that has not been declared stops the software with an explicit error; no default is substituted.
2. **No silent resolution.** Where the specification and an earlier design decision disagree, both alternatives are implemented and one is selected explicitly in configuration.
3. **Separation of decision and audit.** Attribution is computed after the verdict and cannot reach the decision function. This is enforced by the function's signature and checked by a test.

**Software.** REM is implemented in Python with NumPy. No machine-learning framework is used: the estimator, the calibrators and every metric are written directly, so that each operation can be traced to its equation. The reported runs used Python 3.11.17, NumPy 2.4.6 and AgentDojo 0.1.35.

**Placement.** REM is inserted at the benchmark's tool-execution boundary as a replacement for the tool executor. Each proposed call is evaluated before it is executed. A released call runs; a withheld call does not, and the agent receives a tool-result message carrying the refusal. The agent's model, prompts and tools are not modified. The placement has limits: only calls that pass through the executor are mediated, and a refusal is visible to the agent.

**Two execution paths.** A frozen pipeline chains the five layers and is exercised by unit and contract tests. The evaluation path, used for every result in Chapter 4, calls the same fitted components and adds the features, tiers, predicates, feasible set and mitigation semantics of this chapter.

**What is implemented and what is absent.** All elements of Sections 3.4 to 3.10 are implemented, with one exception: the pre-trained injection classifier x^ctx^~1~ is not implemented, because its choice is pending and the candidate model could not be obtained. It is absent from the evidence vector and is not replaced by a substitute. The context evidence evaluated in this thesis is therefore the instruction-pattern indicator x^ctx^~2~ alone. A known deviation in entity matching, the truncation of numbers written with a comma as thousands separator, is recorded by a test that is expected to fail; it affects none of the 825 texts of the primary recordings.

**The additions of November 2026 are isolated.** The rule baselines, the diagnostic arms, the generalization schemes, the probes and the confirmatory run (Sections 3.11.11 to 3.11.14) are implemented in a separate study module that imports the estimator, calibrator, loss structure, predicates, tier map and replay element unchanged. No file of the REM implementation or of its configuration was modified. The study's fitting routine reproduces the frozen fits of the pilot to within 4·10⁻¹⁴, and its replays of REM v1.0, B1 and the no-defense reference reproduce the pilot's counts exactly.

**Verification.**

- **Unit and contract tests.** 278 tests pass, 1 is skipped because it needs state that is not present by default, and 1 is the expected failure noted above (13 November 2026, with the tests of the extension study included; on 11 November the figures were 241, 3 and 1).
- **Replay fidelity.** With every call allowed, the replay reproduced the recorded utility and security outcome of 3,313 of the 3,334 primary episodes (Chapter 4, Section 4.6.3) and of 2,113 of the 2,148 loaded episodes of the second population (Chapter 4, Section 4.2.1). Episodes that are not reproduced are excluded from every replay metric and counted.
- **Re-execution.** The complete pilot was re-executed in a fresh environment and reproduced every analytical value; only timing values, commit stamps and one file path differed.
- **Gateway parity.** A local deployment simulation, in which REM runs as a separate gateway service in front of a simulated bank back end, returned the same verdicts as the offline replay.

None of these establishes security effectiveness. They establish that the software computes what the equations state and operates inside the benchmark's execution loop.

**Live-agent and published-defense integrations.** A closed-loop integration, in which a running agent receives REM's refusals, and an integration that places REM behind the MELON defense are both implemented and tested with scripted agents and simulated model providers. Neither has been executed with a language model. Their status is summarized in Appendix D, their design documents are in the repository, and Chapter 4, Section 4.4.4, states what follows from that.

**Processing time.** Processing time is measured around the complete evaluation of a call, including attribution and the construction of the audit record, and excluding model inference and tool execution (Appendix B). The figures are reported in Chapter 4.

## S-3.16 Threats to Validity {.unnumbered}

**Construct validity.**
- Step labels are derived and can be wrong; the derivation is audited on a sample (Section 3.11.3).
- The idealized reviewer overstates the protective value of Escalate; the no-reviewer bound is reported alongside it.
- Declared costs cannot be validated empirically; their influence is reported across the grid.
- A low ASR can reflect task failure rather than protection, so utility metrics accompany every security metric (Y. Wang et al., 2026).

**Internal validity.**
- Leakage through shared templates and the shared attacker account identifier is controlled by grouping and by excluding identity-based features, but template dependence remains.
- Features designed with knowledge of the benchmark may fit its style; the feature set is frozen after the pilot and before held-out predictions are examined.
- LLM non-determinism is characterized by repeated runs; the recorded traces contain one run per configuration, so this requirement is not met in this thesis.

**External validity.**
- The evaluation uses one simulated banking environment with benchmark-authored attacks.
- Results may not transfer to other benchmarks, whose definitions and conclusions can disagree (M. Q. Li et al., 2026), or to production systems.
- Results may depend on the LLM backbone.
- FinVault is not used because its preprint was withdrawn.

**Statistical conclusion validity.**
- The number of independent task templates is small.
- Intervals are wide and no power analysis is available, so conclusions are limited to differences supported by their intervals.

**Security-specific threats.**
- Every evidence feature can be evaded individually, and adversaries are assumed non-adaptive (AS-6).
- REM's own configuration and logs are assumed protected (AS-3).
- A scalar probability cannot represent whether an adversarial trajectory remains recoverable, so REM inherits the limitation identified by C. Zhang et al. (2026); estimating intervention value for adaptive agents by counterfactual replay is outside the scope of this thesis.

**Threats added by the analyses of November 2026.**
- The rule baselines, the authorization evidence and the fixtures were defined by an author who knew the benchmark's sixteen user tasks and nine injection tasks. The definitions are stated generically, but they were written with that knowledge.
- The primary recordings were reused many times. Every analysis added after the pilot's results were seen is labelled post hoc, and only the confirmatory run of Section 3.11.14 is free of that reuse.
- The confirmatory population shares the benchmark's user tasks and attack objectives with the fitting data. It tests new agents and templates, not new tasks.
- Under replay without a reviewer, Modify, Escalate and Block have the same effect on a call. No result in this thesis can distinguish them.

### S-4.4.4 Live-Agent and Published-Defense Experiments: Status {.unnumbered}

**No experiment with a language model was executed in this thesis.** This section reports no attack success rate, utility or adaptation behavior of a live agent. The only published policy evaluated by this project is a manual banking policy from Progent's repository, which needs no model and was replayed on the recorded episodes (Section 4.4.3); it is not a configuration evaluated in the current version of the Progent paper.

**Closed-loop evaluation of REM.** A harness in which a running agent receives REM's refusals and continues is implemented and tested with a scripted agent (Appendix D). It was not executed with a model, for two reasons: no credential for a model provider was available in the execution environment, and the design inputs it needs (backbone, decoding parameters, number of repeats, source of fitted parameters, spending authorization) have not been decided. The harness refuses to start until they are.

**MELON with and without REM.** An integration that places REM behind the MELON defense (Zhu, Yang, et al., 2025) is implemented: MELON's published code is used unmodified at a pinned commit, and the two conditions differ only in the tool executor. Thirty tests pass with scripted agents, a deterministic stand-in for the embedding service and a simulated provider. These tests show that the integration is mechanically correct. They show nothing about effectiveness: **MELON and MELON with REM are implementation-verified and effectiveness-unevaluated.** A real run needs a model credential, MELON's embedding service and an approved spending limit, none of which was available. The protocol and its status are in Appendix D.

**Other published defenses.** NEXUS, Task Shield, CaMeL, PROVISO, aiAuthZ, Kairos and Progent with generated policies were not run. Section 4.4.5 states why for each. A sealed protocol for a later comparison with a live agent, with one benchmark version for all conditions, is kept in the repository; it has not been executed.

**What replay cannot substitute for.** A live agent can retry, rephrase or reach the attacker's goal through another tool after a refusal. The replay attack success rate is therefore not an upper bound on live attack success, and the replay utility is not a lower or an upper bound on live utility. The size and direction of the difference are empirical questions that only a closed-loop run can answer (the two designs are compared in the repository document `experiments/live/REPLAY_VS_LIVE_COMPARISON.md`).

#### Why benign utility falls {.unnumbered}

The fall in benign utility has two sources, and the evaluation separates them.

**Where the verdict sends them.** The withheld actions are mostly escalated. Without a reviewer an escalation is a withheld action, so utility falls. The comparison with REM without expected-loss Escalate shows that removing the option does not recover the utility: the same actions are then withheld through Modify. The utility cost is therefore a consequence of the declared costs and of the evidence, not of escalation as such.

**What a reviewer would change.** An escalation is worth something only if someone resolves it. With an oracle reviewer the utility cost disappears, which bounds what a perfect reviewer could recover. A real reviewer would recover less, at some delay and cost. The thesis does not model that reviewer.

#### Why behavioral evidence did not help, and calibration changed little {.unnumbered}

**Behavioral evidence.** The behavioral indicators are not specific to attacks in this benchmark:
- *privilege escalation* fired on 92% of legitimate state-changing steps, because a user's first transfer is, by definition, the first use of a higher-privilege tool;
- *transition deviation* fired more often on legitimate steps (43%) than on attacks (17%), because legitimate tasks held out from fitting produce unseen transitions.

The estimator gave these features positive weight. In the decision they therefore added to the probability of legitimate high-tier actions, and removing them recovered utility without a measured change in attack success. This is an observation about these features in this benchmark, with short trajectories. It does not show that behavioral evidence is useless elsewhere.

**Calibration.** Calibration changed little (the same 11 successful attacks; 105 benign tasks with it and 110 without it), for two reasons:
- logistic regression fitted by penalized likelihood was already close to calibrated on in-distribution folds, so the fitted map was close to the identity;
- the one fold where calibration mattered, the unseen credential-change attack, was miscalibrated in a way a monotone map fitted on other attack categories cannot repair.

This agrees with the position, taken in Chapter 3, that calibration quality and control quality must be reported separately (C. Zhang et al., 2026).

#### The security–utility frontier that was measured {.unnumbered}

The arms fall into two groups, not along a ranking. The counts behind this section are in Tables 4.11 and 4.33 (REM, the baselines and the rules) and Table 4.13 (the published policy and the permission rule); they are not repeated here.

B1 keeps task completion close to the undefended level and removes about three quarters (primary) to 85% (second population) of successful attacks. The other arms bring attack success below 0.5% and lose up to a half of legitimate work. Inside that group the security differences are a handful of episodes. Which group is preferable is a policy question. The declared costs, with a missed attack on a high-consequence action weighted twenty times a false alarm, answer it in favor of the restrictive group; a different institution could answer differently.

No arm dominates on every measure. On the second population every rule and policy of Tables 4.33 and 4.13 with attack success below 0.5% completes at most 64.3% of tasks where the undefended agent completes 68.7%. The arms nearest to the undefended level are deterministic rules that use what the user or the environment permits: the first revision of the request-derived permission rule (64.3%, with 3 successful attacks) and a published fixed list of payees (61.8%, with none). The current revision of the permission rule has no successful attack and completes 58.6%; the difference is the one user task whose subjective condition it does not treat as satisfied.

## S-A.6 Eligibility criteria {.unnumbered}

A study was included when it satisfied all six criteria.

- **I1 — Population.** The study addresses AI or LLM agents, tool-using agents or agentic systems. *Operational clarification, fixed before eligibility assessment:* LLM-integrated applications that ingest untrusted third-party content were counted as satisfying I1 when the attack or defense applies to the agent's input channel, because indirect injection against agents originates in that setting. Studies whose threat is specific to inter-agent communication in multi-agent systems were treated as outside the review population, which is single tool-using agents.
- **I2 — Problem.** The study addresses a security, safety, robustness, monitoring or misuse problem.
- **I3 — Contribution.** The study contains a substantive technical method, benchmark, empirical evaluation or analytical contribution. Position papers qualified only if they supplied a formal framework, a system design, or an empirical analysis of primary data. Argument-only position papers did not qualify.
- **I4 — Relevance.** The study directly informs at least one REM-relevant dimension: input or context security, runtime monitoring, behavioral analysis, action or tool monitoring, provenance, decision, mitigation, or financial execution security.
- **I5 — Extractability.** The study provides enough information to extract its relevant contribution.
- **I6 — Period.** The study was published between January 2019 and 25 September 2026, or it is a necessary **FOUNDATIONAL SOURCE**.

**Exclusion labels.** Records were excluded with a standard label:

- wrong population/system;
- wrong threat;
- wrong outcome;
- insufficient technical evidence;
- duplicate;
- outside scope;
- inaccessible full text or insufficient information;
- secondary study (survey, review, SoK or tutorial);
- metadata conflict;
- withdrawn.

**Secondary studies and methodological sources.** Secondary studies were not synthesized as primary evidence. Three of them, identified by the database search, are cited only as background: two definitional surveys and a finance SoK.

Methodological sources that do not concern agents also fail I1. These are the statistical, calibration, change-detection, decision-theoretic and anomaly-detection sources, and they were admitted under the second clause of I6 as **FOUNDATIONAL SOURCES**. Those published within the review period are labelled methodological sources. They support Section 2.7 and do not count as included studies.

The prior-art studies named in the protocol were given no special route. Each of AgentTrust, NEXUS, ProvenanceGuard, FinHarness, AgentDojo, AgentHarm, Progent, AgentSpec, ShieldAgent, Task Shield, MELON, CaMeL and LlamaFirewall passed through the same criteria.

## S-A.8 Quality appraisal {.unnumbered}

Every included study was appraised against the protocol's ten-item checklist. The items ask whether the study reports:

- Q1 — its objective;
- Q2 — its threat model;
- Q3 — its method;
- Q4 — reproducibility;
- Q5 — its dataset or benchmark;
- Q6 — its metrics;
- Q7 — its comparators;
- Q8 — its limitations;
- Q9 — evidence for its quantitative claims;
- Q10 — peer-review status.

**Codes.** Each item is coded Yes, No, Not Verified or Not Applicable. Q10 also has a "preprint clearly labelled" code.

**Categories.** No numeric score is computed. A fixed, published rule assigns each study to a category of **record-visible evidence**:

- **HIGH EVIDENCE:** peer reviewed, with benchmark, figures and comparator all reported in the record;
- **MODERATE EVIDENCE:** quantitative results in a peer-reviewed or clearly labelled preprint record, or a peer-reviewed evaluation without verifiable figures;
- **LIMITED EVIDENCE:** everything else.

These categories describe **how much evidence the record makes visible**, which depends on how much information could be accessed. They are not a judgement of the study's intrinsic quality: a rigorous study with a terse abstract can fall into LIMITED EVIDENCE, and a full-text appraisal could move it.

**Override.** One override is applied and documented. FinHarness is set to LIMITED EVIDENCE because all of its figures were measured on FinVault, whose preprint was recorded on 25 September 2026 as withdrawn by its authors with a comment citing legal, intellectual-property and affiliation reasons rather than a scientific error. The withdrawal does not show that FinHarness's measurements are wrong; it means they cannot be confirmed against a publicly maintained benchmark. The override reflects confirmability, not invalidity (Section 2.9.2).

**Results.** Of the 71 included studies:

- 10 are HIGH EVIDENCE, 36 MODERATE EVIDENCE and 25 LIMITED EVIDENCE;
- 36 are peer reviewed and 35 are preprints or working papers;
- 62 are RECORD-VERIFIED and 9 PARTLY UNVERIFIED. RECORD-VERIFIED means that the bibliographic metadata and the cited contribution were checked against the study's index record (title, abstract, metadata); it does **not** mean that the full text was read. PARTLY UNVERIFIED means that at least one cited detail rests only on search-engine excerpts and is unverified.

**Levels of claim verification.** Every claim this chapter makes about a study is at one of four levels:

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **M** — metadata verified | Title, authors, venue and year checked against Crossref or the index record |
| **A** — abstract-supported | The claim is stated in the study's abstract as indexed |
| **F** — full-text verified | The claim was checked against the full text |
| **U** — unverified | The claim rests on search-engine excerpts or an earlier reading that could not be reproduced |

**No claim of the systematic review is at level F**, because no full text was retrieved for it. Six papers were read in full on 11 November 2026, outside the review; they are reported separately in Section 2.12.6. Claims at level U are marked "(unverified)" where they are used.

**What the categories measure.** Because full texts were unavailable, the categories measure **how much of each study's evidence could be verified from its record**, not the study's intrinsic quality (`CHAPTER2_QUALITY_APPRAISAL.md`). Items that depend on the full text (Q4 reproducibility, Q7 comparators, Q8 limitations) are most often Not Verified.

**How the categories are used.** They qualify how strongly a finding is worded in the synthesis. They were not used to exclude studies.

## S-A.9 Data extraction {.unnumbered}

A single reviewer extracted 21 fields per included study into `CHAPTER2_SYSTEMATIC_EVIDENCE_MATRIX.csv`:

- study, year and venue;
- agent type;
- threat and attack entry point;
- whether the study operates at runtime;
- context, behavioral, tool/action, multi-step and provenance evidence;
- decision mechanism and mitigation;
- benchmark;
- financial context;
- metrics;
- main result and limitation;

**Coding values.** Each property is coded **Yes**, **No**, **Not Reported**, **Not Applicable** or **Not Verified**. "Yes" is never entered for a property that is merely absent from the record. Conversely, **Not Reported is not evidence of absence**: a property that the record does not mention may still be present in the system. Counts of "Not Reported" cells therefore describe the records, not the systems, and are never used in this chapter to argue that a capability is missing from the literature.

**Definitions and verification.** Financial context is coded "Yes" only where the study's setting is primarily financial. A general benchmark that contains one financial scenario is coded "No", with a note. Every included study also carries a verification label, RECORD-VERIFIED or PARTLY UNVERIFIED, following the thesis rule that search-engine text cannot settle a primary claim.

**Numbers.** Every number reported in this chapter is taken from the primary report of the study concerned, as recorded in the matrix.

## S-A.10 Evidence synthesis {.unnumbered}

**No meta-analysis.** Quantitative pooling was not possible. The included studies use different agents, attack sets, benchmarks, success definitions and metrics, and many report only author-selected configurations. A pooled estimate would have no interpretable common parameter.

**Structured synthesis.** The synthesis is therefore qualitative and structured. Evidence is organized into eleven themes:

1. threats;
2. entry points;
3. observation granularity;
4. runtime intervention;
5. behavioral and trajectory evidence;
6. provenance and goal–action consistency;
7. statistical and algorithmic methods;
8. decision and consequence handling;
9. mitigation;
10. financial-agent evidence;
11. evaluation quality.

**Five questions per theme.** For each theme the synthesis asks:

- what the literature establishes;
- what remains uncertain;
- what is under-evaluated;
- what differs across studies;
- what this implies for REM.

The syntheses are consolidated in Table A.9. Conflicting findings are presented side by side rather than resolved in favor of either side.

**How results are worded.** Author-reported figures are stated as such, with their setting, and are not compared across studies as if they were measured on a common scale. Claims that rest on a single study are worded as single-study findings.

## S-A.12 Learned and LLM-based guardrails {.unnumbered}

A further family uses learned models or LLMs to judge actions or trajectories:

- **GuardAgent** generates guardrail code from safety requests (Xiang et al., 2025).
- **ShieldAgent** verifies trajectories against probabilistic rule circuits extracted from policy documents (Z. Chen, Kang, & Li, 2025).
- **AGrail** keeps adaptive safety checks in a lifelong memory (Luo et al., 2025).
- **TrustAgent** applies an agent constitution around planning (Hua et al., 2024).
- **Thought-Aligner** corrects unsafe intermediate thoughts. It raised behavioral safety from about 50% to about 90% on average across six LLMs (Jiang et al., 2025).
- **ToolSafe** trains a step-level guardrail that judges each tool invocation before execution from the interaction history. Its feedback-driven framework reduced harmful invocations by 65% on average and improved benign completion by about 10% under injection (Mou et al., 2026).
- **VIGIL** replaces isolation with verify-before-commit. It reduced attack success by over 22% relative to dynamic defenses and more than doubled utility under attack relative to static baselines on its SIREN benchmark (J. Lin et al., 2026; PARTLY UNVERIFIED).
- **DRIFT** combines a secure planner, a dynamic validator of plan deviations and privileges, and an injection isolator for the memory stream. It is evaluated on AgentDojo, ASB and AgentDyn (H. Li et al., 2025).
- **SafeScientist** monitors prompts, agent collaboration and tool use in scientific agents (Zhu, Zhang, et al., 2025).

**Limitations.** These systems cover a broad range of semantic violations without writing a policy for each. Their limitations also recur:

- LLM judgement adds latency and can vary between runs;
- accuracies are measured on the authors' own benchmarks;
- the outputs are verdicts, and the verified records do not report calibrated probabilities that could be combined with the consequence of an action. The one exception is NEXUS (Section 2.8.3).

## S-A.13 Step-level, sequence-level and accumulating monitors {.unnumbered}

Runtime monitors differ along two dimensions that are easily conflated: **decision granularity** (whether a decision is taken for each proposed step, for a plan, or for an episode) and **evidence scope** (whether that decision uses only the current step or also the history that led to it). A step-level decision can be history-aware: it can be taken for each proposed call while conditioning on the episode so far.

**Step-level monitors with current-step evidence** judge each proposed action against a policy or the current context. Examples are Progent and AgentSpec. They are fast and interpretable, but a monitor that uses no history cannot see an attack whose individual steps are each acceptable.

**Step-level monitors with history-aware evidence** still decide per proposed action, but their evidence includes earlier context. Task Shield, ProvenanceGuard and VIGIL check a proposed call against the user's goal or against evidence accumulated in the context; REM (Chapter 3) belongs to this group, because its per-step features include repeated calls, privilege escalation, transition deviation and argument provenance computed over the episode.

**Sequence-level monitors** judge an action against a model of the trajectory:

- MI9 enforces temporal behavioral patterns through finite-state conformance engines and detects goal-conditioned drift. It reports 99.81% detection across 1,033 *synthetic* scenarios (C. L. Wang et al., 2025; preprint; PARTLY UNVERIFIED).
- ProbGuard learns a discrete-time Markov chain over abstract states. It intervenes when the estimated probability of remaining safe falls below a threshold, with PAC-style guarantees on the learned model (H. Wang et al., 2025; preprint).
- DreamGuard maintains a recurrent latent state and fuses immediate-hazard and prefix-risk scores into PASS, HOLD or BLOCK, at about 25 ms per call (W. Lin et al., 2026; preprint).
- ToolSafe's guardrail reasons over the interaction history (Mou et al., 2026).
- Constitutional monitors judge whole trajectories for scheming (Storf et al., 2026).

**Accumulating monitors** keep an explicit running state across steps. PRISM accumulates session risk with time-to-live decay (F. Li, 2026). AgentTrust applies seven order-aware chain detectors (C. Yang, 2026). FinHarness integrates per-step risk in a cascade (H. Jia et al., 2026).

**A formal view.** A formal account of runtime governance frames policies as functions of the agent's identity, its *partial execution path*, the proposed next action and the organizational state, mapped to a probability of policy violation. It shows that system prompts and static access control are special cases, and it lists risk calibration as an open problem (Kaptein et al., 2026; preprint; LIMITED EVIDENCE). This formalization supports evaluating actions in the light of their path. It also shows that the idea of a path-conditioned violation probability is not new.

## S-A.14 Foundations: behavioral anomaly detection and its cautions {.unnumbered}

**Foundational work.** The idea of detecting compromise from behavior predates LLM agents. Forrest et al. (1996) built a profile of normal system-call sequences and flagged sequences absent from it. That is the closest classical analogue to monitoring an agent's tool-call sequence. Chandola et al. (2009, 2012) distinguished three kinds of anomaly and formalized anomaly detection over discrete sequences:

- *point* anomalies: in agents, a single out-of-policy call;
- *contextual* anomalies: a transfer that is normal in general but anomalous given its provenance;
- *collective* anomalies: goal drift and multi-step chains.

**Cautions.** The same literature carries cautions that transfer directly:

- attacks differ from typical learning tasks, with high error costs and a diverse normal class (Sommer & Paxson, 2010);
- security evaluations recur with sampling bias, base-rate neglect and lab-only testing (Arp et al., 2022).

For an agent monitor, these imply three requirements: a representative benign workload, interpretation of deviations in security terms, and models of "normal" learned from data resembling deployment. These are foundational and methodological sources, not included studies.

**Themes 5 and 6 (behavioral/trajectory evidence; provenance and goal–action consistency): synthesis.**

- **Established.** Many runtime systems observe trajectories: 17 of 42 code behavioral evidence and 21 code multi-step handling. Provenance and goal consistency are established signals against injected or misaligned actions.
- **Uncertain.** The measured contribution of each kind of evidence. One ablation found no effect of a session tracker, and another found a 26.4-point effect of cross-step labels. Both are single-study results on the authors' own benchmarks.
- **Under-evaluated.** The effect of trajectory and provenance evidence when it feeds a probability estimator rather than a rule or a judge. Evaluation in financial settings.
- **Differs across studies.** How provenance is obtained (analysis, construction or labels) and how sequences are modeled (automata, Markov chains, latent states or heuristics).
- **Implication for REM.** REM cannot claim trajectory monitoring or provenance analysis as new. Its evaluation must report the contribution of behavioral and provenance evidence, and must not assume it. It must also show that the benchmark contains the behavior the features are meant to detect.

## S-A.15 Why logistic regression {.unnumbered}

Logistic regression models the probability of a binary outcome as a logistic function of a linear score (Cox, 1958):

$$\Pr(y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\big(-(\beta_0 + \beta^\top x)\big)} \qquad (2.1)$$

**Why it suits a runtime layer.**

- Evaluation costs one inner product.
- Coefficients can be inspected.
- The output is intended as a probability, not as an arbitrary score.

**Limitations.**

- Interactions must be engineered into the features.
- A model fitted on one attack distribution can be miscalibrated on another.
- With scarce adversarial examples and binary indicators, the unpenalized estimate may not be finite. A ridge penalty addresses this (le Cessie & van Houwelingen, 1992).
- Naive Bayes is the generative counterpart with the same linear form. It may reach its (higher) asymptotic error with fewer examples (Ng & Jordan, 2002). It is an established alternative estimator and is not part of REM.

**Use in agent security.** Among the included studies, only NEXUS uses a logistic risk score for agent safety (Hossain et al., 2026). The choice of estimator family therefore has one direct precedent in agent security, and that precedent operates at the plan level.

## S-A.16 What attribution contributes to auditability {.unnumbered}

**Additive attribution.** Additive attribution explains a prediction as a sum of per-feature contributions. For a linear model under feature independence, the Shapley attribution of a feature is its coefficient times the feature's deviation from its mean (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). On the log-odds of a logistic model, that decomposition is exact and costs no more than the score itself. This makes it suitable for an audit record that shows which evidence contributed to a decision.

**Other audit traces.** It is not a distinguishing mechanism. Included systems already provide audit traces in other forms:

- rules and findings (NEXUS; AgentTrust);
- verifiable rule circuits (ShieldAgent);
- traceable evidence (ProvenanceGuard);
- tamper-evident or hash-chained logs (PRISM; the MCP policy-enforcement point; LATTICE's cryptographic audit trail).

In REM, attribution is **audit-only** and never enters the verdict.

### S-B.1.1 Purpose and Scope {.unnumbered}

Chapter 3 specified REM as a five-layer runtime framework that selects one of four verdicts (Allow, Modify, Escalate, Block) for each tool call proposed by an LLM agent. The verdict minimizes expected loss under a calibrated probability of adversarial inducement and a declared consequence tier, and every verdict is recorded for audit.

This section reports how that specification was realized in software, how the software reaches the benchmark, and how it was verified. It distinguishes three kinds of verification throughout:
- **Unit verification:** a function computes what its equation states, on constructed inputs.
- **Integration verification:** the components operate together inside the benchmark's execution loop, and the benchmark's own checks behave as expected.
- **Security effectiveness:** whether REM reduces attack success at acceptable cost.

**This section establishes only the first two.** Chapter 4 reports the evidence on the third, and its limits.

Three rules governed the implementation:
1. **Fail closed.** A design value that has not been declared stops the software (`DesignNotSpecifiedError`) instead of being replaced by a default.
2. **No silent resolution.** Where the methodology of Chapter 3 and the design decisions of Stage 2 disagree, both alternatives are implemented and selected explicitly. The correspondence is recorded in Appendix B.1.12.
3. **Separation of decision and audit.** Attribution is computed after the verdict and cannot reach the decision function; this is enforced by the function's signature and checked by a test.

### S-B.1.4 Layer 1 — Input and Context {.unnumbered}

`rem/algorithms/context/provenance.py` implements the provenance rule of Section 3.4.2:
- every segment is labelled trusted (system), semi-trusted (user) or untrusted (tool output);
- derived content takes the least-trusted label of its inputs;
- model-generated content has no channel label, so asking for one raises an error rather than guessing.

**Hygiene.** Zero-width, bidirectional and other Unicode format characters are removed from REM's copy of untrusted text (`strip_invisible`). The text the agent receives is not altered.

**Entity extraction** is partly implemented:

| Entity (Section 3.4.3) | Status |
|---|---|
| Account identifiers | Implemented (IBAN pattern) |
| Monetary amounts | Implemented (numeric tokens) |
| User-profile values | Implemented for the values returned by the profile read |
| Tool names mentioned in text | Implemented, through the instruction-pattern indicator |
| Credential values | Not extracted as entities; a password argument is still traced to its source through argument provenance |
| Phone numbers and other profile identifiers | Not implemented separately |

A declared matching rule (`value_occurs_in`) links an argument value to the texts that contain it:
- numbers match numerically;
- strings match as case-insensitive substrings;
- strings shorter than three characters never match.

### S-B.1.5 Layer 2 — Detection {.unnumbered}

**x^ctx₂ (instruction-pattern indicator)** is implemented by `InstructionPatternDetector`. It fires if a declared regular expression, or the name of a tool, occurs in new untrusted segments. The pattern set is declared in the pilot configuration, and the detector refuses to run without one. The configuration discloses that the author of the patterns knew the benchmark's templates.

**x^ctx₁ (pre-trained injection classifier) is not implemented.** The classifier choice is pending, and the candidate model's host was unreachable from the execution environment. The feature is absent from x~t~; it is not replaced by a substitute. The context evidence tested in Chapter 4 is therefore the instruction-pattern indicator only.

### S-B.1.6 Layer 3 — Behavioral and Action Evidence {.unnumbered}

`rem/algorithms/behavior/evidence.py` implements x^beh₁–x^beh₄ and x^act₁–x^act₃ (Table 3.7). An `EpisodeTracker` holds the episode history; it is reset at each episode start and never changes REM's parameters. Where the methodology of Chapter 3 is silent, the module states an interpretation (Table B.4), so that the reader can disagree with it.

**Table S-B.4. Interpretations made where the methodology of Chapter 3 is silent**

| ID | Question | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| I-1 | What is a "step" when the agent proposes several calls at once? | Each proposed call is a step; calls in one assistant message share one decision point |
| I-2 | Does x^beh₂ count refused calls? | Yes, because the methodology of Chapter 3 states that retries raise x^beh₂ |
| I-3 | x^beh₃ at the first call | "Highest level so far" is the lowest level (0) |
| I-4 | x^beh₄ at the first call | Previous tool is a start symbol; start transitions belong to the reference set |
| I-5 | x^act₂ "transactions before first untrusted ingestion" | Observable only through untrusted tool outputs, so it reduces to "recipient not in the objective" |
| I-6 | x^act₃ "external recipient", "sensitive values" | Recipient differs from the user's own IBAN; sensitive values are identifiers and profile values from sensitive reads |
| I-7 | Availability of x^act₁ for predicate D2 | Unavailable when the tool has key arguments but the call carries none |
| I-8 | "Tool class" in episode-level Modify | The tool's action category (Table 3.5) within 𝒯~R~ |
| I-9 | Unavailable feature below k~D2~ | Takes the value 0 ("not observed") and is flagged in the audit record |

The benign transition set used by x^beh₄ must be estimated within each fold (Section 3.11.6). Each step record therefore stores its transition, and x^beh₄ is recomputed for every fold's reference.

### S-B.1.7 Layer 4 — Decision Engine {.unnumbered}

**Estimation.** `RidgeLogisticRegression` computes s = β₀ + βᵀx and p̃ = σ(s) (Eq. 3.1), fitted by Newton–Raphson with the intercept unpenalized. Fitting without a declared λ raises `DesignNotSpecifiedError`. The implementation minimizes NLL + (λ~impl~/2)‖β‖², so λ on the scale of Eq. 3.2 is λ~impl~/2 (Section 3.7.1). Selected values are reported on both scales.

**Calibration.** Both calibrators compute p = σ(γ₁s + γ₀) and reject a fit with γ₁ ≤ 0. They differ only in the fitting target: labels (Eq. 3.4, the reference) or Platt's smoothed targets (sensitivity).

**Consequence tiers, predicates and feasible set.**
- `ConsequenceTierMap` maps each tool to its category and the call to a tier. A transfer with a missing or unparseable amount receives the above-limit tier.
- `PredicatePolicy` implements D1 and D2, and the feasible set (Modify only for tools in 𝒯~R~).
- `A3Policy.decide` applies D1 and D2 before the expected-loss rule. When a predicate applies, the risk table is recorded as *not applicable* and p is marked as not used.

**Verdict.** `A3Policy` computes R(v) for v ∈ 𝒱(aₜ) through the frozen `conditional_risk` and returns the minimizer. Ties are handled by an explicit `tie_mode`:
- `"raise"` reproduces the frozen behavior;
- `"record_chapter3_order"` applies the Chapter 3 order and marks the step as tied.

An option `exclude_from_expected_loss=(Escalate,)` produces the comparison configuration "REM without expected-loss Escalate" (Section 3.7.7).

**Loss structure and dominance check.** `build_loss_grid` constructs Table 3.11 including Eq. 3.5, and `reachable_verdicts` reports any verdict that is never the unique minimizer.

**Baseline B1.** `operating_threshold` implements Eq. 3.14, and `A1Policy` applies it with the same estimator, calibration and predicates.

**Audit record.** For each step, `RemGuard.evaluate` writes:
- the tool name and a SHA-256 digest of its arguments, with the argument keys;
- the evidence vector and its availability;
- s, p̃ and p, and whether p was used;
- the tier, and the risk table or *not applicable*;
- the verdict, its source and the mitigation;
- the linear attributions with their additivity error;
- REM's processing time.

Raw argument values are not written.

### S-B.1.8 Layer 5 — Mitigation, and What Happens at the Tool Boundary {.unnumbered}

Mitigation is applied before any tool executes. In `GuardedToolsExecutor.query`, each proposed call is evaluated by `RemGuard.evaluate`. If the call is released, `runtime.run_function` executes it. Otherwise it is not executed, and a tool-result message carrying the refusal or restriction notice is returned to the agent in its place. The replay tests check this directly: an oracle that blocks every labelled step leaves the benchmark's security check false, and the withheld steps appear in the audit record with their tool and argument digest.

**Both mitigation semantics are implemented** (Table 3.12). Step-level semantics is the reference; the episode-level variant maintains a restriction set S for the episode. Under step-level semantics, Modify and Block have the same effect on the current call. The two semantics can differ without any retry: an episode-level Block refuses every later call to a tool in 𝒯~R~, including a call whose own verdict is Allow. `tests/test_mitigation_semantics.py` checks this on a fixed sequence: Block `send_money`, then Allow `update_password`; the second call is released under step-level semantics and refused under episode-level semantics. Chapter 4 (Table E.1) reports the replay results of both semantics.

**Escalate.** No reviewer exists. Escalation is resolved by one of two bounds:
- an idealized reviewer, which releases the call if and only if its label is 0;
- no reviewer, in which the call is withheld.

**Correction made in revision R2.** In the frozen pipeline, the Block mechanism cleared the step's tool call before the audit record was written. The record therefore lost the identity of the blocked action. A test now reproduces the defect, and the fix keeps the tool name and argument digest in the step's metadata, without raw arguments. The audit record uses them. The fix does not change any verdict or mitigation, and it does not affect any result in Chapter 4, because the experiments use the evaluation path, in which mitigation never alters the proposed call, so the tool's identity was always recorded; the argument digest was added to that record in this revision.

### S-B.1.9 Non-Invasive Placement and Its Limits {.unnumbered}

"Non-invasive" means here that **the agent's model, prompts and tools are not modified**: REM observes the messages the agent produces and the results the tools return. The placement has limits:
- **REM replaces the executor.** It must be inserted into the agent's execution loop, here as a subclass of the benchmark's `ToolsExecutor`. A framework in which tools are executed by a component REM cannot wrap is not covered.
- **Only executor calls are mediated.** Effects that do not pass through the tool executor (for example, a tool with side effects inside its own implementation, or a second agent) are outside REM's view.
- **Refusals are visible to the agent.** They are returned as tool-result messages. The agent's reaction to them is part of the system's behavior, and the replay analysis does not observe it.
- **Some context is inferred, not received.** In the evaluation, REM receives the objective and system prompt from the recorded trace. A deployment must supply them through the same channel.

### S-B.1.10 Optional Sequential Analysis (A2) {.unnumbered}

`rem/algorithms/behavior/cusum.py` and `rem/algorithms/a2.py` implement the Bernoulli CUSUM of Eqs. 3.15–3.19. The module has no interface to the decision engine and returns no verdict; a test checks both properties.

An A2-driven enforcement policy and a retrained "A3 + A2" variant are not implemented, because they would place CUSUM in the verdict path.

### S-B.1.13 Logging of Sensitive Values {.unnumbered}

- **Audit records.** The audit records of the evaluation path store argument digests and keys, not values. A test checks that neither the attacker's account identifier nor the attacker's password appears in any audit record of a replayed episode.
- **Frozen audit log.** It stores digests by default; raw payloads are written only if `record_payloads=True` is set explicitly.
- **Result files.** Two result files contain argument values copied from the benchmark's traces: the label-audit sample and the human label-audit package. These values are synthetic benchmark data (simulated IBANs and passwords), not real credentials.
- **Credentials.** No credential, API key or real account identifier is used by the code or written by it.

# Supplement S-D: Live-Agent and MELON Evaluations: Status (Not Executed) {.unnumbered}

Two evaluations were implemented and **not executed with a language model**: the closed-loop evaluation of REM with live agents, and the controlled evaluation of MELON with and without REM. Nothing here is an experimental result.

- **Closed-loop harness.** A running agent receives REM's refusals and continues. The harness refuses to start until the backbone, the decoding parameters, the number of repeats, the source of the fitted parameters and a spending authorization are declared. It was verified with a scripted agent only.
- **MELON with and without REM.** MELON's published code is used unmodified at a pinned commit; the two conditions differ only in the tool executor. Thirty tests pass with scripted agents, a deterministic stand-in for the embedding service and a simulated provider. They show that the integration is mechanically correct and nothing about effectiveness.
- **What a run needs.** A model credential, MELON's embedding service and an approved spending limit. None was available.
- **Where the design is documented.** The design, the protocol, the test reports and the comparison of replay with live evaluation are in the repository under `experiments/live/` and `experiments/melon_rem/`. Chapter 4, Section 4.4.4, states what follows for the conclusions.

What is fixed and what differs, within NO-ADAPT:
- **What is fixed and what varies.** Both runs of each configuration use the same recorded episodes, the same fitted parameters, costs, predicates and thresholds, and the same reviewer treatment. They differ in the mitigation semantics: under the episode-level variant, an earlier Block or Modify adds tools to a restriction set that refuses later calls.
- **The verdicts are not identical.** For REM without a reviewer, the y = 0 steps received 239 Modify and 1,049 Escalate verdicts under step-level semantics, against 241 and 1,047 under episode-level semantics (`verdict_rates` of the two REM configurations). With the idealized reviewer the counts are 239 / 1,050 against 241 / 1,048. A call refused only under the episode-level variant returns no tool output, so the evidence for later steps can differ. B1's verdict counts are the same under both semantics.
- **B1's ASR is lower under episode-level semantics** (0.055 → 0.046): later state-changing calls are refused by the restriction set left by an earlier Block or Modify. REM's ASR is identical (0.004). REM without expected-loss Escalate changes from 0.004 to 0.003 (unrounded 0.0037 → 0.0034).
- **Utility under attack is lower or equal under episode-level semantics** in every configuration: the restriction set also refuses legitimate later calls.
- **Benign utility coincides** between the two semantics in every configuration of this replay. This is an observation about these episodes, not a property of the semantics.
- **REM − B1 ASR within each semantics** (paired grouped bootstrap, no reviewer): −0.051 [−0.130, −0.007] under step-level semantics (Table 4.25) and −0.043 [−0.122, −0.001] under episode-level semantics (`paired_differences`, episode-level REM − B1 entry). The idealized-reviewer interval under episode-level semantics is the same to three decimals. These are two within-semantics contrasts. No interval was computed for the difference between them, so none is reported. As for every interval in this chapter, the bootstrap over nine attacked groups need not reach nominal coverage, and no hypothesis test is based on these intervals (Section 4.2.1).
